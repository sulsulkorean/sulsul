Korean small talk phrases for beginners work best when you stick to a few reliable topics: where you're from, what you think of Korea, and what you're studying. Ask **어디서 왔어요?** (*eodiseo wasseoyo?*), "Where are you from?" and answer with **미국에서 왔어요** (*migukeseo wasseoyo*), "I'm from the US." Add **한국어 공부하고 있어요** (*hangugeeo gongbuhago isseoyo*), "I'm studying Korean," and most conversations open up on their own.

> - Ask where someone is from: **어디서 왔어요?** (*eodiseo wasseoyo?*), "Where are you from?"
> - Tell them your country: **미국에서 왔어요** (*migukeseo wasseoyo*), "I'm from the US." — swap in your country name.
> - Show enthusiasm for Korea: **한국 음식 너무 좋아해요** (*hanguk eumsik neomu joahaeyo*), "I really love Korean food."
> - Admit you're still learning: **한국어 공부하고 있어요** (*hangugeeo gongbuhago isseoyo*), "I'm studying Korean."

## What topics work for Korean small talk?

Three topics carry almost any first conversation: origin, impressions of Korea, and language. They're low-risk because they invite the other person to share too, and most Koreans enjoy hearing that someone has made the effort to study.

| Topic | Say this |
|---|---|
| Where are you from? | 어디서 왔어요? (*eodiseo wasseoyo?*) |
| I'm from Australia | 호주에서 왔어요 (*hojueseo wasseoyo*) |
| Love Korean food | 한국 음식 너무 좋아요 (*hanguk eumsik neomu joayo*) |
| Studying Korean | 한국어 공부하고 있어요 (*hangugeeo gongbuhago isseoyo*) |
| First time in Korea? | 한국 처음 왔어요? (*hanguk cheoeum wasseoyo?*) |

One quick note: **좋아해요** (*joahaeyo*) means "I like it" in general, while **좋아요** (*joayo*) agrees with something right in front of you — "it's good." Both work in small talk; just pick one and stay consistent.

## How does a first-conversation exchange actually go?

Understanding the reply is the moment most people get stuck, not the opener. Here's a realistic exchange:

You: **어디서 왔어요?** — *eodiseo wasseoyo?* — "Where are you from?"  
Them: **저는 서울 사람이에요.** — *jeoneun Seoul saramieyo.* — "I'm from Seoul."  
You: **서울이 정말 좋아요!** — *Seouli jeongmal joayo!* — "Seoul is really great!"

After the opening, they'll often ask **한국어 잘 하네요!** (*hangugeeo jal haneyo!*), "Your Korean is good!" — the natural reply is **아직 배우고 있어요** (*ajik baeugo isseoyo*), "I'm still learning." That keeps the conversation going without overselling your level.

## What usually goes wrong in Korean small talk?

The most common mistake is going quiet after the first exchange because you don't know what to say next. Prepare one follow-up question in advance so the silence doesn't catch you off guard.

A second mistake is treating small talk as a grammar test. Koreans in casual settings are forgiving of imperfect sentences — what matters is that you tried. Use **-요** (*-yo*) endings throughout; they're polite without being stiff, and the right default for anyone you just met. If you're still working out when to use formal versus casual Korean, the guide on [greeting people with the right politeness level](/greet-korean-politeness-levels) covers exactly that.

I've seen learners memorize twenty phrases and still go blank when a Korean person responds. The problem is almost never vocabulary — it's that they've never said the phrases out loud with someone actually listening.

## How do you keep the conversation going?

Once you've exchanged origins, shift to impressions or plans. **한국에 얼마나 있을 거예요?** (*hanguke eolmana isseul geoyeyo?*), "How long will you be in Korea?" opens up a natural follow-up. If they ask what you've seen, say the name of a place and add **정말 좋았어요** (*jeongmal joasseoyo*), "it was really great." You don't need a full opinion — one adjective is enough.

If you want to continue the conversation another time, try **카카오톡 있어요?** (*KakaoTok isseoyo?*), "Are you on KakaoTalk?" It's the most common way Koreans exchange contact information in casual settings.
