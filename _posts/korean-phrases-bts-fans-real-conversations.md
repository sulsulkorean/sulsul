When all seven BTS members completed their mandatory military service and reunited in 2025, Weverse was flooded with Korean fan messages summing up years of emotion in a few words. **보고 싶었어요** (*bogo sipeoosseoyo*), "I missed you," appeared thousands of times — and it is not just a fan phrase. It is one of the most natural things to say in Korean when you have not seen someone for a while, whether the person is an idol or a friend.

> - Tell someone you missed them: **보고 싶었어요** (*bogo sipeoosseoyo*), "I missed you."
> - Thank someone for their support: **힘이 됐어요** (*himi dwaesseoyo*), "You gave me strength."
> - Say you waited for them: **기다렸어요** (*gidaryeoosseoyo*), "I waited for you."
> - Express pure enthusiasm: **너무 좋아요** (*neomu joayo*), "I love this so much."

## What do Korean fans say that works in everyday Korean?

The phrases fans reach for are usually the ones that express a feeling they cannot hold back. Those tend to be short, direct, and emotionally loaded — which is exactly what makes them work outside a fan context too.

**보고 싶었어요** is the canonical example. It literally means "I wanted to see you" — and in Korean, that is how you say "I missed you." It works for an idol returning from the military, a friend back from a year abroad, or a grandparent you have not visited in months.

**힘이 됐어요** (*himi dwaesseoyo*) — "you gave me strength" — is the phrase fans use after a hard year. In everyday Korean, it is what you say to a teacher who got you through an exam, a coworker who covered for you, or a friend who kept checking in when you were struggling.

| Fan phrase | Real-life use |
|---|---|
| 보고 싶었어요 | Greeting someone you haven't seen in a long time |
| 힘이 됐어요 | Thanking someone who supported you through something |
| 덕분에 잘 지냈어요 | Saying you're doing well thanks to someone |
| 기다렸어요 | Any situation where you waited for a person or moment |

## How does a reunion exchange sound in Korean?

Fan messages on Weverse look like short emotional bursts. Real conversations have a back-and-forth. Here is how the exchange plays out when you are genuinely happy to see someone:

You: **보고 싶었어요!** — *bogo sipeoosseoyo!* — "I missed you!"  
Them: **나도요. 잘 있었어요?** — *nadoyo. jal isseosseoyo?* — "Me too. Have you been okay?"  
You: **덕분에 잘 지냈어요.** — *deokbune jal jinaesseoyo.* — "I've been doing well, thanks to you."

**덕분에** (*deokbune*) is one of those words that sounds more meaningful than it translates. It means "thanks to you" or "because of you" in a positive sense — and Koreans use it sincerely, not as a throwaway phrase. When a fan says **덕분에 힘을 냈어요** (*deokbune himeul naesseoyo*), "thanks to you I found strength," that is not hyperbole in Korean culture. It is a direct statement of feeling.

## When do fan phrases cross into awkward Korean?

The line is usually register — how casual versus how formal the phrase is.

**보고 싶었어요** ends in **-아요/-어요**, the standard polite ending, which means it is appropriate with almost anyone. The fully casual version — **보고 싶었어** (*bogo sipeoosseo*) — drops the polite ending and only belongs with close friends who have already invited that level of familiarity. Most fans write the casual version to idols because the parasocial closeness feels real; copying that form with someone you actually know and recently met is too fast.

The safest rule: keep the **-요** (*-yo*) ending on everything until the other person drops it first. That applies whether you borrowed the phrase from a fan post or a textbook.
