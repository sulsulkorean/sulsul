Korean greetings change with the relationship and the setting. The three forms beginners hear most are **안녕** (*annyeong*, casual "Hi"), **안녕하세요** (*annyeonghaseyo*, everyday polite "Hello"), and **안녕하십니까** (*annyeonghasimnikka*, formal polite "Good day/Hello"). They are related, but they are not interchangeable decorations.

The safest default with an unfamiliar adult is the everyday polite form. Casual speech is for a relationship in which both people accept it; being the same age does not establish that automatically. The formal version belongs to deliberately formal situations, not every conversation with a boss or older person.

## The three levels beginners need

Korean has more speech styles than these three, but this practical map covers most beginner greetings:

| Speech style | Greeting | Revised Romanization | Typical context |
|---|---|---|---|
| **해체** (*haeche*), casual | **안녕** | *annyeong* | Close friends, siblings, children; only when casual speech fits |
| **해요체** (*haeyoche*), polite | **안녕하세요** | *annyeonghaseyo* | Unfamiliar adults, acquaintances, neighbors, most daily service interactions |
| **하십시오체** (*hasipsioche*), formal polite | **안녕하십니까** | *annyeonghasimnikka* | Speeches, broadcasts, ceremonies, military-style contexts, very formal introductions |

Politeness is not a simple ladder based only on age or job title. Familiarity, role, workplace culture, and the formality of that moment all matter. A younger employee may use polite **-요** sentences with a manager during an ordinary conversation, then use formal **-습니다** language in a presentation.

## Greeting a close friend

Use casual language only after the relationship supports it. **안녕** can mean either "Hi" or "Bye," although other parting expressions are often clearer.

You: **안녕! 오랜만이야.** — *annyeong! oraenmaniya.* — "Hi! Long time no see."  
Them: **그러게. 잘 지냈어?** — *geureoge. jal jinaesseo?* — "I know. Have you been well?"  
You: **응, 잘 지냈어.** — *eung, jal jinaesseo.* — "Yeah, I've been well."

Every ending here is casual. **응** (*eung*) is the casual "yes"; do not use it as your default response to a customer, teacher, or unfamiliar adult. A learner does not need to force casual language to prove closeness. Friends may continue using polite speech for personal or stylistic reasons.

## Greeting someone politely for the first time

For a classmate you have just met, a neighbor, or a new colleague in a relaxed workplace, everyday polite speech is usually natural.

You: **안녕하세요. 저는 마야예요.** — *annyeonghaseyo. jeoneun mayayeyo.* — "Hello. I'm Maya."  
Them: **반가워요. 저는 지훈이에요.** — *bangawoyo. jeoneun jihunieyo.* — "Nice to meet you. I'm Jihun."  
You: **저도 반가워요.** — *jeodo bangawoyo.* — "Nice to meet you too."

**반가워요** is warm and polite. For a more formal first meeting, choose **처음 뵙겠습니다** (*cheoeum boepgetseumnida*), "It is a pleasure to meet you." The verb **뵙다** is a humble form of "to see/meet," lowering the speaker relative to the person being met.

You do not need a separate Korean equivalent for English "good morning" in every encounter. The standard greeting works throughout the day. In a household or close relationship, a morning check-in such as **잘 잤어요?** (*jal jasseoyo?*), "Did you sleep well?" may be more natural.

## Opening a formal event

The formal greeting has a crisp, public-facing tone. It works well when addressing an audience or representing an organization.

You: **안녕하십니까. 오늘 발표를 맡은 리나입니다.** — *annyeonghasimnikka. oneul balpyoreul mateun rinaimnida.* — "Good day. I'm Rina, today's presenter."  
Them: **네, 반갑습니다.** — *ne, bangapseumnida.* — "Yes, it's nice to meet you."  
You: **먼저 참석해 주셔서 감사합니다.** — *meonjeo chamseokhae jusyeoseo gamsahamnida.* — "First, thank you for attending."

All three turns use **하십시오체**. In an ordinary office hallway, repeating this level can sound like an announcement. A polite daily greeting is often enough; formal speech becomes appropriate when the event, role, or institutional norm calls for it.

## Greetings for a specific moment

Korean speakers often greet through a relevant question or acknowledgment rather than repeating one all-purpose hello. These expressions still require context:

- **오랜만이에요** — *oraenmanieyo* — "Long time no see." Use it with someone you actually know but have not seen recently.
- **잘 지내셨어요?** — *jal jinaesyeosseoyo?* — "Have you been well?" The honorific **-시-** respects the subject.
- **식사하셨어요?** — *siksahasyeosseoyo?* — "Have you eaten?" This can be a warm check-in around mealtime, but it is also a real question and not a mandatory greeting for every Korean person.
- **다녀오셨어요?** — *danyeoosyeosseoyo?* — "Are you back?" / "Did you have a good trip out?" Say it when someone returns.
- **어서 오세요** — *eoseo oseyo* — "Welcome." Staff or hosts say this to someone arriving; a customer does not normally say it back.
- **수고하셨습니다** — *sugohasyeotseumnida* — "Thank you for your hard work." This acknowledges completed effort and can close a work interaction.
- **다음에 봐요** — *daeume bwayo* — "See you next time." A polite parting line.
- **안녕히 가세요** — *annyeonghi gaseyo* — "Goodbye" to the person leaving.
- **안녕히 계세요** — *annyeonghi gyeseyo* — "Goodbye" to the person staying.
- **안녕히 주무세요** — *annyeonghi jumuseyo* — "Good night" to someone you address respectfully.
- **잘 가** — *jal ga* — "Bye / Go well" to a close person who is leaving.
- **또 봐** — *tto bwa* — "See you again" in casual speech.

Notice that **식사하다** is written without a space: **식사하셨어요?** Revised Romanization also keeps **siksa** and the following verb together as *siksahasyeosseoyo*. Accurate spacing helps learners recognize the phrase as a conjugated verb.

## Titles, bows, and what not to assume

Korean often uses a title plus **-님** instead of "you": **선생님** (*seonsaengnim*, "teacher") or **팀장님** (*timjangnim*, "team leader"). In a workplace, following the name-and-title pattern used by colleagues is safer than inventing a family-like term.

A small nod or bow commonly accompanies a greeting, but there is no single angle or ritual required for every encounter. Observe the setting and respond naturally. Eye contact, distance, and physical contact vary by person and situation; avoid turning tendencies from dramas into cultural rules.

Do not use **오빠** (*oppa*), **언니** (*eonni*), **형** (*hyeong*), or **누나** (*nuna*) merely because someone is older. These terms encode the speaker's gender, the other person's gender, relative age, and a close or accepted relationship. A title or name is safer until you know what the person prefers.

## Choose a greeting in five steps

1. **Identify the relationship.** Is this a stranger, acquaintance, close friend, family member, colleague, or audience?
2. **Read the setting.** A café chat, office presentation, and family home can call for different speech even with the same person.
3. **Default to polite speech.** Use **해요체** when you lack enough information.
4. **Match the whole sentence.** Do not open formally and then switch accidentally to a casual question.
5. **Follow the other person's lead carefully.** If someone says **말 편하게 하세요** (*mal pyeonhage haseyo*), "Please speak comfortably," you can clarify whether they are inviting casual speech.

## Frequently asked questions

### What politeness levels should a beginner learn first?

Learn casual **해체**, everyday polite **해요체**, and formal polite **하십시오체**. Focus first on producing the polite style consistently; recognize the others so you understand friends and formal announcements.

### How do I greet a Korean friend?

If you already use casual speech together, **안녕** is natural. If the friendship is new or you still use polite endings, keep the everyday polite greeting. Friendship does not require an immediate switch.

### Is the formal greeting best for every older person?

No. It can sound ceremonial in an ordinary interaction. Everyday polite speech usually shows appropriate respect; titles, honorific **-시-**, and the surrounding setting also contribute.

### What is a natural Korean morning greeting?

The standard polite hello works in the morning. With someone close, **잘 잤어?** (*jal jasseo?*) means "Did you sleep well?" With polite speech, use **잘 잤어요?** (*jal jasseoyo?*).

### Why do Korean coworkers sometimes skip a direct hello?

People may use a context-specific acknowledgment, a title, or a comment about the immediate task. That does not create a rule that greetings are unnecessary; it shows that greetings can be expressed in more than one fixed phrase.
